Review Articles: Energy Storage & Electrocatalysis

Energy Storage HER OER ORR MXenes & MOFs

Overview

The transition to a sustainable energy future depends on two complementary capabilities: storing energy efficiently, and converting it cleanly through electrochemical reactions. Alongside my experimental and computational research, I have co-authored a series of peer-reviewed review articles that survey the advanced materials driving both of these frontiers — with a particular focus on emerging two-dimensional MXenes, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), metal selenides, metal sulfides, and their composites.

These reviews connect materials chemistry to device performance, critically assessing how structure, composition, and interface engineering govern behavior across supercapacitors, batteries, and the three key electrocatalytic reactions — the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), oxygen evolution reaction (OER), and oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) — that underpin clean hydrogen, fuel cells, and metal–air technologies.

Energy Storage

Supercapacitors

Supercapacitors store charge physically and near-instantly, giving them very high power density, rapid charge–discharge, and exceptional cycle life — often hundreds of thousands of cycles with little degradation. They store energy in two ways: electric double-layer capacitance, where ions accumulate at the electrode surface, and pseudocapacitance, where fast, reversible surface redox reactions add extra charge. Their main limitation is lower energy density than batteries, so the central materials challenge is boosting stored energy without sacrificing power or lifetime. Two-dimensional materials such as MXenes are especially attractive here: their metallic conductivity, large accessible surface area, and rich surface chemistry make them outstanding supercapacitor electrodes.

Batteries

Batteries store energy chemically, delivering much higher energy density than supercapacitors, which is why they power everything from phones to electric vehicles. Charging and discharging shuttle ions (Li+, Na+, Zn2+, and others) between two electrodes while electrons flow through the external circuit. Performance is set by how much charge the electrodes can hold, how fast ions move in and out of them, and how well the structure survives repeated cycling. The reviews examine how MXene- and MOF-derived materials — and their composites with metal sulfides and selenides — improve capacity, rate capability, and stability across lithium-, sodium-, and other next-generation battery chemistries.

Electrocatalysis for Clean Energy

Electrocatalysts lower the energy barrier of the reactions at the heart of clean-energy devices, making them faster and more efficient. A good catalyst is judged by its overpotential (the extra voltage needed to drive the reaction — lower is better), its Tafel slope (how efficiently rate increases with voltage), and its long-term stability. Three reactions are central:

Hydrogen Evolution Reaction (HER)

The HER is the cathode reaction in water electrolysis, producing clean hydrogen gas (2H+ + 2e− → H2). Platinum is the benchmark catalyst but is scarce and expensive, so a major goal is designing earth-abundant alternatives — such as transition-metal carbides, sulfides, selenides, and phosphides on conductive 2D supports — that approach platinum-like activity at a fraction of the cost.

Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER)

The OER is the anode reaction in water splitting (2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e−). Because it involves a four-electron transfer, it is kinetically sluggish and usually the efficiency bottleneck of the whole electrolyzer. Developing active, durable, non-precious OER catalysts — often based on transition-metal oxides, hydroxides, and MOF-derived structures — is essential for practical, low-cost hydrogen production.

Oxygen Reduction Reaction (ORR)

The ORR is the cathode reaction in fuel cells and metal–air batteries, where oxygen is reduced to complete the circuit (O2 + 4H+ + 4e− → 2H2O). Its slow kinetics limit fuel-cell efficiency, and again the aim is to replace precious-metal catalysts with heteroatom-doped carbons, single-atom catalysts, and MXene/MOF-based materials that deliver high activity and durability.

Materials in Focus

  • MXenes — 2D transition-metal carbides/nitrides with metallic conductivity, tunable surface chemistry, and hydrophilicity, ideal for both storage and catalysis.
  • Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) — highly porous, tunable frameworks and the hollow/derived nanostructures made from them.
  • Metal sulfides & selenides — redox-active phases that pair with 2D supports to boost capacity and catalytic activity.
  • Interface & surface engineering — doping, hybridization, and AI/ML-guided design used to optimize performance across all applications.
